L11 Database technology and privacy

Lesson 11, Privacy. Discussion, posted 15 November 2026.

The prompt

Research the tension between privacy and database technology and argue how it will or should play out, with citations, then reply to two classmates.

My post

The idea that people my age don’t value privacy doesn’t match the research. I think the real tension is that databases have become too many and too hidden for any one person to manage, so the answer has to come from rules on companies, not more effort from users.

Start with the evidence. Hargittai and Marwick (2016) ran focus groups with university students and found that young adults understand the risks of sharing online and do take some steps to protect themselves, yet feel that information is out of their hands once it is shared. The authors call this “online apathy”. It looks like not caring, but it is closer to giving up. Survey data shows the same thing across all ages: in a Pew Research Center study, 73% of Americans said they have little to no control over what companies do with their data, and 67% said they understand little to nothing about it (McClain et al., 2023).

My own experience this week fits that pattern. For our other L11 assignment, “What do companies know about you?”, I reviewed my Google account and found that my search and browsing history was being saved with auto-delete switched off, and that more than 39 outside apps were connected to my account.

There is a fair argument on the other side. Databases give us real benefits, like fraud alerts on a bank card, useful recommendations and faster healthcare, and people can read the privacy policy and opt out. But Solove (2013) argues that this “privacy self-management” model fails in practice, because nobody can read and weigh hundreds of policies, and the harm often comes from combining data across databases, which no single consent form covers.

So how should the tension play out? I think the burden should move from the user to the organisation. That means privacy-protective defaults (auto-delete on, not off), limits on how long data is kept and on selling it to others, and laws like the EU’s GDPR that apply whether or not a user clicks “agree”. We don’t ask drivers to inspect their own brakes at the factory; we set safety standards for car makers. Databases that hold our lives deserve the same approach.

In short, my generation hasn’t stopped valuing privacy. We’ve grown up in a system where protecting it alone is unrealistic, and the fix is better rules, not better users.

References

Hargittai, E., & Marwick, A. (2016). “What can I really do?” Explaining the privacy paradox with online apathy. International Journal of Communication, 10, 3737–3757.

McClain, C., Faverio, M., Anderson, M., & Park, E. (2023, October 18). How Americans view data privacy. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/10/18/how-americans-view-data-privacy/

Solove, D. J. (2013). Privacy self-management and the consent dilemma. Harvard Law Review, 126(7), 1880–1903.

My replies to classmates

To a classmate who argued people should decide for themselves

I liked how honest you were about wanting TikTok to know what you like. I feel the same way about my recommendations, and I think most people do even if they won’t admit it. Your Shin and Kim (2025) point was the most useful part for me: people are fine with data collection when it happens openly, and the problem starts when it’s hidden.

I’d push a little on your conclusion though. You say people should make these decisions for themselves, but I’m not sure transparency is enough. A Pew survey found that 67% of Americans understand little to nothing about what companies do with their data (McClain et al., 2023), even though privacy policies already exist. If most of us skip the policy, does more transparency really give us control? Or should apps come with safer default settings, so people who never check are still protected?

Reference

McClain, C., Faverio, M., Anderson, M., & Park, E. (2023, October 18). How Americans view data privacy. Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/10/18/how-americans-view-data-privacy/

To a classmate who imagined campus systems being joined together

Your campus hypothetical was the part that stuck with me. Each of those systems (meal plan, library, gym, Wi-Fi) seems harmless on its own, and the risk only shows up when they’re joined together. I think that’s the strongest point in your post, because no privacy policy I’ve ever clicked “agree” on has explained what happens when databases get combined.

I came to a similar conclusion in my post, that the problem is structural and not generational. For our other L11 assignment I checked my Google account and found over 39 outside apps connected to it, which fits your point that nobody can realistically keep track.

One question on privacy by design: many of these companies make their money from the data itself. Do you think they would build privacy in voluntarily, or does it only work if regulation like GDPR forces it?
