L04 Hardware evolution and e-waste

Lesson 4, Hardware. Discussion, posted 24 September 2026.

The prompt

Write about two of several hardware topics. I chose how hardware has evolved and what that costs the environment.

My post

Hardware Evolution: It's hard to picture how far hardware has come until you compare the numbers. Intel's first microprocessor, the 4004 from 1971, had about 2,300 transistors, while Apple's M3 Max chip from 2023 has around 92 billion. This follows Moore's Law, the idea that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. Smaller transistors are faster and use less power, which is why devices keep getting quicker and more efficient. Storage has improved in a similar way. SSDs have no moving parts, so laptops can be thinner and boot up much faster than they could with hard drives. What surprised me is that this progress is slowing down, since transistors are now only a few nanometres wide and hard to shrink any further.

Environmental Impact: The downside of hardware improving so quickly is that people replace their devices more often. The World Health Organization reports that e-waste is the fastest-growing type of solid waste, and less than a quarter of it is formally collected and recycled. It also points out that informal recycling can expose workers, including children, to toxic materials like lead. With my minor in Supply Chain, I think the biggest change has to come from how devices are designed. Phones with glued-in batteries are hard to repair, so people replace them instead. The EU now requires phone batteries to be user-replaceable by 2027, and I think more governments should follow its lead.

My replies to classmates

To a classmate who wrote about RAM

I liked that you picked RAM, since it makes a big difference to how a computer feels day to day. One thing I found interesting is that extra RAM only helps up to a point. Once you have enough for what you're running, adding more won't make much difference. Your AI point connects well too. A lot of new laptops and phones now come with a separate chip just for AI tasks, called an NPU, so those features can run on the device itself.

To a classmate who wrote about how the components depend on each other

I liked your point that all the components depend on each other. It's why a fast CPU still feels slow if there isn't enough RAM. Your point about devices sitting unused stood out to me too, since I have a couple of old phones in a drawer. I think more people would recycle if trade-in programmes made wiping your data easier.
