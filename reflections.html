Reflections

Every entry ends with a reflection: what I did, what was hard, what I'd change and how it connects to the course. This page brings them together.

Looking back on the semester

I'll write this once the course finishes in December, looking back across all fourteen lessons.

Every reflection

L00 Semester learning and portfolio plan

On day one I picked GitHub Pages with a site written in plain HTML and CSS, before I really knew either. I would make the same choice again. Website builders tend to produce sites that all look alike, and GitHub let me make something that feels like mine.

It also ties back to the course more directly than a builder would. The HTML we tried in Lesson 9 is the same language every page of this site is written in, and the whole site is kept on GitHub, so every change is saved.

L00 Setting up enterprise software

This felt like admin at the time, but it set up the tools I used for everything after it. As a finance major I already use PowerPoint and Excel all the time, and in IST 110 I went back to them for the hardware dissection deck, the OSINT presentation and plenty of write-ups.

The useful surprise was Acrobat Pro. It didn't appear in Creative Cloud under my Penn State account, so instead of faking a screenshot I recorded that I only had the basic version. It showed me that enterprise software is really about licences: what you can use depends on what your account is entitled to, not just on downloading the app.

L00 Subscribing to zyBooks

I picked Chapter 13, Business Issues, as the chapter I knew most about, and that turned out to be right. I had covered a lot of the same ground in my MIS classes and other IST courses.

The chapter that taught me the most was privacy. It has the most real-life consequences, especially now that AI is built into almost everything we use. My Lesson 11 work made it personal when I found more than 39 outside apps connected to my Google account. Starting the course by naming my strongest area made it easier to see where I actually grew.

L01 Virtual information treasure hunt

The search taught me as much as the article. Searching "information overload" on its own mostly returned pieces about email and social media habits. Only pairing it with "decision making" and filtering to peer-reviewed articles got me to Roetzel (2019).

His inverted U-curve stuck with me: more information helps a decision until you hit your limit, and after that it makes the decision worse. I see that most at school, where there is always more coming in than I can handle at once. My answer has been to build systems to track it all, often with AI helping. That is the core of information science too: finding information is easy, and judging and organising it is the hard part.

L01 Introducing myself and Information Systems

I chose Information Systems because of my internship, where credit approvals stalled while people chased information across three systems that didn't connect. After this semester I still think it is the right fit for me, given my major and what I study, but I would describe it differently now.

It sits between two skill sets: the hard, analytical data skills of IST, and the finance side of turning that data into a conclusion someone can act on. Writing about the discipline's limits was harder than writing about its strengths, because I was criticising the field I want to work in.

L02 Binary representation

I had done binary in high school, so the idea wasn't new, but I'm not the strongest with numbers and converting by hand still took concentration. When working through 156 to 10011100 by taking away powers of two, one misread digit changes the whole answer, and nothing tells you it is wrong.

What made it interesting this time was the bigger point behind it. Binary has no meaning on its own: 01000001 is the number 65 or the letter A depending on the encoding standard used to read it.

L02 Binary statement

I decoded the three strings by hand, and they came out as Hello, This is a binary code and I love data. The last bytes, 00001010, weren't a letter, and I only realised that when I checked them against an ASCII table. They are the line feed character.

That moment summed up the topic for me. The way computers store information is simple, just 1s and 0s, but it is also complex, because the same bits carry formatting and control instructions as well as the text you see. In my replies I explained why similar codes trip people up, since A and a differ by just one bit.

L03 Computational thinking infographic

Using one example, making lunch for five friends, let me show the four ideas as one process instead of four separate definitions. Decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithms only really make sense when you see them working together, and a sandwich order is something everyone gets.

AI wasn't allowed on this one, so I kept the design simple. Looking at it now, I'd cut a lot of the text and let the visuals do more of the explaining.

L03 Data analytics: my muddiest point

Honestly, early on I treated discussions as a box to tick. My post here was short and my replies were mostly quick agreements. The content was fine, and thinking of it as what happened, what will happen and what should we do actually cleared up predictive versus prescriptive for me.

From Lesson 5 my posts got longer and more structured. Looking back, this post is a fair snapshot of where I started.

L04 Hardware dissection lab

The MacBook Air is my own laptop, so I wanted to see what's actually inside it, and the Switch is basically a computer squeezed into a handheld, which made it a good contrast.

The MacBook surprised me most. There's no fan or heatsink at all, and the RAM sits inside the M2 chip, so it can never be upgraded. That's a trade-off I didn't know I'd made when I bought it: thinner and quieter, but no way to extend its life later.

L04 Hardware evolution and e-waste

The numbers made Moore's Law real for me: about 2,300 transistors in 1971 against around 92 billion in a 2023 chip. But the part that stuck was the e-waste side.

Learning that under a quarter of e-waste is formally recycled made me think about the old phones sitting in my drawer. I'd rather keep devices longer and use trade-in schemes. With my Supply Chain minor, I think the real fix is designing devices to be repaired, like the EU's replaceable battery rule.

L05 Choosing the best operating system

The interior design firm was the hardest call. macOS suits creative work, so I expected to recommend it, but Revit and 3ds Max only run on Windows, and that settled it.

It felt a lot like advisory work in finance: you start from the client's constraints and budget, not the product you happen to like. The nonprofit was similar. Windows won on cost and on staff already knowing it, not on being the best system in the abstract.

L05 Learning portfolio milestone 1

The plan and the reality didn't fully match. I didn't stick to the OneDrive folders and checklist as closely as I meant to, and in the end I pulled most of my work straight from Canvas.

The structure held up, though. The site's sections are close to the outline I wrote in September, which tells me the planning that mattered was deciding what the portfolio needed to show, not how I'd file things along the way.

L05 A love letter to YouTube

My first version read like an essay, so I cut it down twice until it sounded like something I'd actually say. Short and a bit cheeky turned out to be harder to write than long and formal.

Writing it also made me notice how much of what I know came from free YouTube videos rather than a classroom. That's a bigger role for one app than I'd ever really thought about.

L06 Python: making the computer decide

Pasting my code added spaces, which caused an IndentationError, and a missing colon stopped the whole program before a single line ran. That was the main lesson: in Python, spacing and punctuation carry meaning, while Excel is much more forgiving about how you type things.

Once that clicked, writing my own if, elif and else program was straightforward, and my confidence went from 3 out of 5 to 5.

L06 Programming languages

I used to think an easy language meant a forgiving one. Python changed that for me. It's easy to read, but it's strict about structure, as my own IndentationError showed. So now I'd say easy means readable, not lenient.

The FFmpeg example was a good reminder of the other end: when speed really matters, people still drop down to assembly and trade readability for control.

L07 CODIS cold case

Taking a side was harder than the research. The Schuett case shows how much a DNA database can do, solving a crime nearly twenty years later. But I still argued that DNA from people who are never convicted should be removed automatically, without them needing a court order.

Arguing against the system after showing what it achieved felt uncomfortable. I think that's the point of the privacy question, though: something being useful doesn't settle whether it's fair.

L07 Music Genome Project

Only two of my five songs were actually by The Police. Down Under got picked for its reggae-style rhythm, not who made it, which showed me Pandora matches on sound rather than artist.

What surprised me most was that trained musicologists score every song by hand, taking 20 to 30 minutes each. The station also drifted towards general 80s hits pretty quickly, so the matching is clever, but it isn't the same as knowing what I'd pick myself.

L07 SQL basics

At first I thought I'd made a mistake when INSERT and DELETE failed. Then I found out the W3Schools editor is now read-only, so those statements can't work there, and I explained that in a comment on my submission.

The SELECT side felt familiar. It's the same filtering and sorting I do in Excel for finance, just on much bigger tables, and it ties straight into the MySQL work I'm doing in IST 210 this semester.

L07 Excel macros

In my finance work I've mostly used formulas rather than VBA. The consolidation macro is something I'd actually use for recurring reports, where the same sheets get stacked every week.

The catch I'd keep in mind is the one I found in my research: if one sheet gets an extra column, the macro can quietly break and nobody notices. So I'd check the layout before trusting the output.

L08 Open source intelligence (OSINT)

In under five minutes I found 2,140 Penn State hostnames through public certificate logs, plus a domain registration going back to 1986. That was the eye-opener.

It was uncomfortable to realise an attacker could build the same list just as easily, which is exactly why organisations run OSINT on themselves. It also showed me that public isn't the same as fair game. Just because information is out there doesn't mean every use of it is OK.

L09 HTML basics

A practice page is a single file. A whole site needs navigation, the same styling on every page, a folder structure, hosting and upkeep. Seeing this portfolio built showed me that most of the work is in the structure, not the tags.

The four tags from this exercise, a heading, a paragraph, a link and an image, are still the building blocks of every page here.

L09 JavaScript basics

The if and else logic clicked quickly because it's the same idea as in Python. Events took me longer. The difference between code that runs as soon as the page loads and code that waits for a click is simple once you see it, but it isn't obvious at first.

A place I'd actually use this is a simple calculator on a page, like a loan repayment tool.

L09 The Internet and the Web

The road and traffic comparison is what finally made the Internet and the Web make sense to me as two different things.

Two things went wrong this week, though. I forgot to post my replies to classmates, and I lost 15 points on the subject criterion for not backing my points with enough evidence from the course content. The post leaned on outside sources and my own experience more than the zyBook, so next time I would tie my points back to the course reading as well.

L10 Security infographic: the WannaCry attack

I picked WannaCry because it's a real, well-known attack that shows how ransomware spreads through unpatched systems. The bit that stuck with me is that Microsoft had released a patch two months before the attack.

Getting the design right took three attempts. The early versions looked too AI-made, so I switched to a Canva template.

L11 What do companies know about me?

Seeing more than 39 connected apps I'd forgotten about was the unsettling part. Each one was a quick "Sign in with Google" that made sense at the time, and together they add up to a lot of access I never really thought about.

My uncommon name also means a search brings up almost nothing but me. The fixes I wrote down were to turn on auto-delete and clear out apps I don't use.

L11 Database technology and privacy

I still think the burden should be on companies. Finding 39 apps I'd agreed to without remembering shows that users can't realistically manage this alone, however careful they mean to be.

We set safety standards for car makers instead of asking drivers to check their own brakes, and I think data should work the same way. In my replies I pushed the same point: transparency alone isn't enough if nobody reads the policy.
