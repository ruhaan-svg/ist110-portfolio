Reflections

Every entry ends with a reflection: what I did, what was hard, what I'd change and how it connects to the course. This page brings them together.

Looking back on the semester

I'll write this once the course finishes in December, looking back across all fourteen lessons.

Every reflection

L00 Semester learning and portfolio plan

On day one I picked GitHub Pages with a site written in plain HTML and CSS, before I really knew either. I would make the same choice again. Website builders tend to produce sites that all look alike, and GitHub let me make something that feels like mine.

It also ties back to the course more directly than a builder would. The HTML we tried in Lesson 9 is the same language every page of this site is written in, and the whole site is kept on GitHub, so every change is saved.

L00 Setting up enterprise software

This felt like admin at the time, but it set up the tools I used for everything after it. As a finance major I already use PowerPoint and Excel all the time, and in IST 110 I went back to them for the hardware dissection deck, the OSINT presentation and plenty of write-ups.

The useful surprise was Acrobat Pro. It didn't appear in Creative Cloud under my Penn State account, so instead of faking a screenshot I recorded that I only had the basic version. It showed me that enterprise software is really about licences: what you can use depends on what your account is entitled to, not just on downloading the app.

L00 Subscribing to zyBooks

I picked Chapter 13, Business Issues, as the chapter I knew most about, and that turned out to be right. I had covered a lot of the same ground in my MIS classes and other IST courses.

The chapter that taught me the most was privacy. It has the most real-life consequences, especially now that AI is built into almost everything we use. My Lesson 11 work made it personal when I found more than 39 outside apps connected to my Google account. Starting the course by naming my strongest area made it easier to see where I actually grew.

L01 Virtual information treasure hunt

The search taught me as much as the article. Searching "information overload" on its own mostly returned pieces about email and social media habits. Only pairing it with "decision making" and filtering to peer-reviewed articles got me to Roetzel (2019).

His inverted U-curve stuck with me: more information helps a decision until you hit your limit, and after that it makes the decision worse. I see that most at school, where there is always more coming in than I can handle at once. My answer has been to build systems to track it all, often with AI helping. That is the core of information science too: finding information is easy, and judging and organising it is the hard part.

L01 Introducing myself and Information Systems

I chose Information Systems because of my internship, where credit approvals stalled while people chased information across three systems that didn't connect. After this semester I still think it is the right fit for me, given my major and what I study, but I would describe it differently now.

It sits between two skill sets: the hard, analytical data skills of IST, and the finance side of turning that data into a conclusion someone can act on. Writing about the discipline's limits was harder than writing about its strengths, because I was criticising the field I want to work in.

L02 Binary representation

I had done binary in high school, so the idea wasn't new, but I'm not the strongest with numbers and converting by hand still took concentration. When working through 156 to 10011100 by taking away powers of two, one misread digit changes the whole answer, and nothing tells you it is wrong.

What made it interesting this time was the bigger point behind it. Binary has no meaning on its own: 01000001 is the number 65 or the letter A depending on the encoding standard used to read it.

L02 Binary statement

I decoded the three strings by hand, and they came out as Hello, This is a binary code and I love data. The last bytes, 00001010, weren't a letter, and I only realised that when I checked them against an ASCII table. They are the line feed character.

That moment summed up the topic for me. The way computers store information is simple, just 1s and 0s, but it is also complex, because the same bits carry formatting and control instructions as well as the text you see. In my replies I explained why similar codes trip people up, since A and a differ by just one bit.
